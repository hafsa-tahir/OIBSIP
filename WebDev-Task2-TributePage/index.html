01 · SMILJAN, CROATIA

A Mind Wired for Invention

Born in 1856 in the village of Smiljan, Tesla showed an early gift for visualizing entire machines in his head before ever touching a tool. He claimed he could build and test an invention mentally, down to its wear points, without drawing a single line — a habit that would later fill his notebooks with designs years ahead of the workshops that could build them.

He studied engineering in Graz and Prague, and it was there, staring at a sparking DC motor, that he first imagined a rotating magnetic field — the idea that alternating current, rather than fighting itself, could be made to spin in continuous, elegant motion.

02 · THE CURRENT WAR

Alternating Current, Set Loose

In 1884 Tesla arrived in New York with little more than a letter of introduction to Thomas Edison. Their partnership was brief; Tesla's belief in alternating current put him at odds with Edison's DC empire. He struck out on his own, and by 1888 his AC induction motor patents had been licensed by George Westinghouse.

The rivalry became public spectacle. Tesla's system won the contract to light the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago, and two years later it was harnessing Niagara Falls itself — proof that power could travel far from where it was made.

03 · WARDENCLYFFE, LONG ISLAND

A World Without Wires

Tesla's ambitions outgrew the power grid. In 1898 he demonstrated a radio-controlled boat to a startled New York audience — one of the first working pieces of what we would now call robotics. He believed the same principle could send not just signals but electricity itself through the air.

Construction began on the Wardenclyffe Tower in 1901, meant to broadcast wireless communication and power across the Atlantic. Funding collapsed before the vision could be tested at scale, but the tower's silhouette remains a symbol of ideas that arrived before their infrastructure did.

04 · LEGACY

What Still Runs on His Ideas

Tesla died in New York in 1943, largely out of the public eye and with several projects unfinished. Yet the alternating current system he championed became the backbone of the electrical grid the world still runs on — from the outlet behind your desk to the substations that feed entire cities.

His fingerprints are on more than power lines: radio, remote control, and wireless energy transfer all trace a line back to patents he filed a century ago. The tribute you're reading is delivered, fittingly, over a wireless signal he would have recognized as the fulfillment of his own unfinished tower.
